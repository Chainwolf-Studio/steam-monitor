Whether you’re looking after your survivors, following a lead into dangerous territory, or building a more capable shelter, there are milestones to pursue along the way.
Keeping supply runs worthwhile
A well-stocked shelter is a comforting sight. Keeping it that way should still give you reasons to venture outside.
We’ve made light adjustments to loot on the early maps to keep exploration and scavenging relevant as you approach the mid-game. The aim is to make supplies worth searching for, even once your shelter is well established.
Loot remains deliberately generous overall. These are small adjustments to the campaign’s resource flow, with enough room for players to keep their campaigns moving.
More breathing room on Hard
Hordes bring pressure, danger, and a serious interruption to whatever you had planned for the day. On Hard difficulty, though, they were arriving too frequently, sometimes once a day.
Their spawn frequency has been adjusted to give survivors more breathing room between encounters. The update also includes navigation fixes.
Behind the balance
There’s a lot going on beneath the surface of the shelter economy.
A growing population consumes more resources. Enemy numbers change with difficulty and horde spawns, affecting how much loot you can collect. Dismantling items adds materials to the mix, while trader exploits can push the economy further off balance.
These systems are interconnected: even reducing the frequency of hordes changes the amount of loot available during a campaign.
For that reason, we’re taking a measured approach. Resources are generous by design to help ensure campaigns remain possible to finish.

## 25. Too Angry to Clean

- **AppID:** 5390660
- **Title:** Making it official: I don't use AI in development!
- **Published:** 2026-10-24T08:29:39Z
- **Author:** (not provided)
- **URL:** https://store.steampowered.com/news/app/5390660/view/699905228356458088

### Announcement text

Lately I've been noticing a lot of hysteria around AI. I get it, a lot of devs plugged neural networks into their engines and started making games faster. For some people it's a lifeline, for others it's a one-way ticket, and not to a happy place. I'm tired of all of it, so I decided to speak up. Sure, you'll say I found the perfect place for it, a dev diary.
Why don't I plug AI into my engine? The answer is pretty simple. As a developer I'm pretty conservative. And I'm also scared of breaking things. When a system works, I'd rather not touch it unless it's buggy. And what does AI do? It basically takes over your project. You have to let it in, and there's no going back after that. The files you've been grinding on for 15 hours a day are now in its hands, and it can do whatever it wants with them. For a conservative guy like me that's a nightmare.
The only way I'm okay with using it is as a chat. Some questions get really tricky, especially with physics, and asking in a chat is often faster than digging through the docs. Same goes for tricky parts of C++ code.
Who do you think actually uses AI? Probably newcomers who just discovered game dev as a "niche" and are hyped about all the money they could make. And then there are people at companies who are forced to use it. Every day I read posts on Reddit from people saying they have to switch to AI completely and can't even review all that code. Though to be fair, plenty of experienced devs use it too, just as a tool.
On one hand it's progress, but on the other hand a lot of people I know have lost their jobs. These are professional developers with 10 and 20 years of experience. I'm luckier because I'm a marketer by trade and dev has only been a hobby for the last few years, but I have a feeling AI might replace me soon too.
I hope I made it clear that AI isn't used in Too Angry to Clean or my second game, Island of Ash, and explained why. That's it for this topic for now, and I'll be sending all the non-believing haters to this post. In the next one I'll talk more about the game itself.
