- **Published:** 2026-09-15T06:30:14Z

### Announcement text

Once the UI skeleton was there and the game had its real content sitting inside actual widgets, the next obvious question was: alright then, what can the player do ? That is where developments, policies and actions properly entered the picture. Because cities - or nodes, as they’re internally called - have developments which the player can unlock and build up. Those developments unlock actions. A printing press allows the player to publish pamphlets. A public opera house might make the masses happy, but the elites won’t like rubbing their fancy silks against a commoner’s rough hide coat - as for the clergy, well, they never condoned that buffoonery from the start. So we started implementing developments first, then policies, then actions, along with all the usual lovely baggage: requirements, costs, outputs, modifiers, limits, effects, edge cases, and the eternal question of whether a system is genuinely interesting, or whether it only looks that way because it’s, well… yours. Naturally, this also meant another round of revisions. Because the moment these systems became clickable, every stubbornly lingering vague design assumption became a practical problem. Costs had to make sense, effects had to be traceable, dependencies had to behave, and the whole thing had to be readable enough that the player could tell what was going on. Of course, because we want the player to be comfortable, we just added detailed tooltips for every single thing you hover on… Ok, we’ll be honest: we did it so we can more easily spot bugs. This was also the point where the game loop began to show itself clearly enough that it started feeling like an actual strategy game. And, obviously, the moment that happened, everything became both more interesting and more difficult, because it quickly became obvious just how much balancing this game was going to need, especially since this particular design relies to a great extent merely on good ‘ol numbers. (April 2026)

Once our great web of interrelations between works, projects, developments and policies was fully loaded up, looking pretty and shiny in Miro, it had to actually be implemented. What does that cover as of now. 

Trading ships will spawn from ports and head to a friendly port, usually carrying commodities. Some factions favor their own ports while others favor trading with other factions.

Patrols will spawn around ports, engaging the player to check for contraband if reputation is not high enough.

Pirates will spawn in open waters and attack or flee from the player, depending on flotilla power. A lone corvette will never bother your destroyer.

Every pass (5 min), the ports update. Produced items get added to their inventory while needed items will be consumed, thus affecting price. If a port has high amounts of produced items, it will lower the price and allow the player to buy in bulk. If it lacks needed items, they will pay a premium for a resupply, which will also earn you some reputation for helping them out.

The shipwrights will have a new ships for sale, some which may even be on discount. And new privateers will appear in the taverns, where the player can recruit them for their flotilla. Privateers usually run modified ships so you can find armored transport ships, glass cannon corvettes and many more combinations. The notice boards update more frequently, since people come and go with requests.

What's in works? 

Patrols will attack or chase away pirates in their route, clearing up the way for the transports.

Pirates will attack trading ships following the same logic of risk.

Whenever that happens, the player can intervene and engage the pirates, helping the traders escape and earning you some reputation in the process. 

Special convoys carrying greater loot will occasionally spawn.

Hunter flotillas will be dispatched to search and destroy the player if the reputation is low with the faction.

And more to come!

Would you like to know more about different aspects of the game? Leave a comment and I'll try to do a detailed overview in the next devlog. 

Thank you for your time and support!

- **Title:** Devlog #5: The Arena Opens - First Look at Combat
- **Published:** 2026-08-26T13:40:31Z
- **Author:** Andrei H
- **URL:** https://steamstore-a.akamaihd.net/news/externalpost/steam_community_announcements/1842212951296928
- **GID:** 1842212951296928

### Announcement text

Greetings Lanistas! Bringing the arena to life A lot has changed since our last update and there is still plenty we want to improve, but this is the first time we feel ready to open the arena doors and let you see where combat is heading. Over the past months, we've put a lot of work and iteration into the arena. We knew Gladiatorum couldn't become the game we want it to be unless the spectacle of its fights matched our ambitions and vision for the title. Until now, we've shown you many of the systems surrounding the life of a lanista - managing your ludus, developing your gladiators, scouting opponents and preparing for the battles ahead. But there is one part of Gladiatorum: Sacramentum that we have deliberately kept behind closed doors. The fights themselves. Today, for the first time, we're ready to show you what happens when your decisions finally reach the sands. And this is only one part of the much larger management game we're building around it. More than watching two gladiators fight Combat in Gladiatorum is not about directly controlling your fighter. Your work begins before he ever enters the arena. Study your opponent. Compare the matchup. Choose the gladiator you believe is right for the fight. Decide how he should approach the battle through his stance and fighting style. Then send him onto the sands and live with those decisions. Once the fight begins, you remain the lanista on the sidelines. You can read the battle as it develops, change your tactical stance and shout commands to your gladiator, but ultimately, he is the one inside the arena. His attributes, training, condition, morale, fighting style, opponent and the decisions you've made all come together in the simulation unfolding in front of you. We'd love to hear what you think This is an important milestone for us, and we're especially interested in your first impression of the fights. Does the arena feel like something you would want to send your gladiators into? Let us know in the comments. And if you enjoy what you see, sharing the video or showing Gladiatorum to someone who might enjoy this kind of game helps us enormously. Thank you for following the development of Gladiatorum: Sacramentum . The arena is finally open!

### Announcement text

Development on Pinnacle Point has been moving forward steadily, with several major parts of the game coming together. One of the biggest recent additions is the vehicle system . The goal was never to make the car feel like a light arcade vehicle. It needed weight, suspension, momentum, and that slightly unwieldy feeling of driving a proper heavy machine through the world. It’s now getting much closer to that. The game's lighting has also been completely reworked . This ended up affecting the atmosphere of pretty much every area, so alongside it I’ve also been rebuilding and improving the vegetation and environmental presentation. The map itself is now fairly fleshed out , with most of the major areas taking shape and starting to feel like parts of one coherent place rather than separate development environments. There's still plenty to refine, but Pinnacle Point is increasingly starting to look and feel like the game I've had in my head. More soon.
